## Where do laws exist to translate process content to local languages?

**Important** - this article is not legal advice. You need to do your own research and use this content at your own risk.

Some countries fine you for English-only workplace documents. Others just recommend translations. This reference maps out which jurisdictions require workplace content in local languages - and which ones are fine with English only.

### What types of workplace content are subject to language requirements?

- **Employment contracts** - the most common rule, in France, Belgium, Romania, Slovenia, Saudi Arabia and others below.
- **Job offers and application forms** - for example in Quebec and the UAE.
- **Policies, handbooks and work rules** - for example in Quebec, Japan and Thailand.
- **Training and safety instructions** - for example OSHA training in the US, and safety information in Slovenia.
- **Any document an employee needs to do the job** - France covers these, and Saudi Arabia covers the employer's instructions to workers.
- **Workplace notices and posters** - for example in California, New York and under the US Family and Medical Leave Act (FMLA).

## North America

### Canada (federal)
**Requirement level:** Mandatory (federal institutions)
**Applies to:** Federal institutions
**Requirements:** The Official Languages Act makes English and French the languages of work in all federal institutions, and their employees can use either one. In the National Capital Region and other prescribed regions, regularly and widely used work documents must be available in both languages. [1]

### Canada - Quebec
**Requirement level:** Mandatory
**Applies to:** All employers in Quebec
**Requirements:** The Charter of the French Language (Bill 96) makes French the language of work. Written communications from an employer to its employees must be in French. An employee can ask for private written communications in another language, and the employer can then use that language alone. Documents about conditions of employment (such as an anti-harassment policy or employee handbook), training documents, offers of employment, transfer or promotion, and job application forms must be available in French. Versions in other languages are allowed if the French version is of similar quality and equally accessible. A negotiated employment contract can be in any language both sides agree on. [2] Employers had until June 1, 2023 to translate existing application forms, documents about conditions of employment, and training documents. [3] The worker's right to work in French applies whatever the number of employees in Quebec. [4] The separate francization rules cover employers with 25 or more employees from June 1, 2025. [2] Non-compliance can result in fines. [5]

### Canada - New Brunswick
**Requirement level:** Mandatory (provincial institutions)
**Applies to:** Provincial institutions, and private companies providing a service on behalf of the provincial government
**Requirements:** New Brunswick's Official Languages Act applies to provincial institutions such as departments, Crown corporations, hospitals and police services, which must offer service in both English and French. Private companies don't have to offer bilingual service unless they provide a service on behalf of the provincial government. [6]

### United States (federal)
**Requirement level:** Mandatory for OSHA training and some federal notices; no blanket requirement otherwise
**Applies to:** Private and public sectors (for OSHA and federal notices)
**Requirements:** Training required by OSHA standards must be presented in a way employees can understand. If an employee doesn't speak or comprehend English, instruction must be in a language the employee can understand. [7] Where a significant portion of a workforce isn't literate in English, the employer must give the FMLA general notice in a language those employees can read. [8] The EEOC presumes that a rule requiring employees to speak only English at all times violates Title VII. A rule that applies only at certain times needs a business necessity, and the employer should tell employees about it. [9] Beyond these, a patchwork of federal laws can require notices in languages besides English. [10]

### United States - states
**Requirement level:** Mandatory for specific notices (varies by state); encouraged in some states for other documents
**Applies to:** Private and public sectors (state-level requirements)
**Requirements:** California's Labor Code Section 2810.5 wage notice must be given in the language the employer normally uses to communicate employment-related information to the employee. [11] A California employer whose workforce at any facility is more than 10% non-English-speaking must also post the state's workplace discrimination and harassment notice in the appropriate languages. [12] New York, Illinois, Virginia and Massachusetts require some notices and posters in languages besides English, while Ohio, Indiana, Maryland and Washington encourage employers to provide translation or guidance. [10]

### Mexico
**Requirement level:** No statutory language rule for contracts; translation required for foreign-language documents in labor court
**Applies to:** Private and public sectors
**Requirements:** Mexico's Federal Labor Law has no article requiring the employment contract to be in Spanish. Documents filed with a labor court in a foreign language must come with a translation, and the court appoints an official translator, who presents and confirms the translation under oath within five days (Article 809). [13]

### Other North America
**Requirement level:** Mostly recommended or preferred languages
**Applies to:** Employers
**Requirements:** Polsinelli groups many Latin American countries among those with recommended or preferred languages for employment documents. [10]

## Europe

### France
**Requirement level:** Mandatory (French required for employment documents)
**Applies to:** Employers
**Requirements:** Under the Toubon Law, an employment contract must be written in French (Labor Code Article L.1221-3), and an employer can't use terms that aren't in French against the employee. Labor Code Article L.1321-6 extends this to any document containing obligations for the employee or information needed to do the job. [14] The rule applies even where the company's working language is different, and if a contract exists in two languages and they conflict, the French version prevails. [15]

### Belgium - Flanders (Dutch-speaking)
**Requirement level:** Mandatory (Dutch only)
**Applies to:** Employers whose employee works from an office in the region
**Requirements:** Contracts must be drafted in the official language of the region where the employee's operational office is, which is Dutch in Flanders. A contract in another language, such as English, is generally void, except in some international situations under strict conditions. Translations can help employees understand, but only the Dutch version is binding. [15] The nullity can't be used to the employee's disadvantage. [16]

### Belgium - Wallonia (French-speaking)
**Requirement level:** Mandatory (French)
**Applies to:** Employers whose employee works from an office in the region
**Requirements:** In Wallonia the required language is French. Documents that breach the rule are null, though no administrative or criminal fines are added on top. An additional language chosen by the parties is allowed, but the French version stays the reference. [16]

### Belgium - Brussels (bilingual)
**Requirement level:** Mandatory (French or Dutch depending on employee)
**Applies to:** Employers in the Brussels region
**Requirements:** Documents for staff must be in Dutch for Dutch-speaking staff and in French for French-speaking staff. Breaking this rule doesn't make a document null. Instead, the employer has to replace it with a version in the correct language. [16] Employees in Brussels may choose the language for employment-related communications. [15]

### Luxembourg
**Requirement level:** No set language (mutual understanding required)
**Applies to:** Employers
**Requirements:** An employment contract must be concluded in a language both parties understand. A contract in a language the employee doesn't know can be declared invalid for lack of consent. [17]

### Germany
**Requirement level:** No explicit requirement
**Applies to:** Employers
**Requirements:** There's no obligation to draw up employment contracts in German, and contracts can be fully in English. A German translation is needed in case of litigation or if an authority requires one. [18]

### Netherlands
**Requirement level:** No specific law (check employee understanding)
**Applies to:** Employers
**Requirements:** Dutch law doesn't require an employment agreement in Dutch, and English agreements are common practice in international employment. The law firm Buren advises checking that the employee fully understands the terms and providing a translation in case of doubt. [19]

### Spain
**Requirement level:** Spanish required, according to one law firm
**Applies to:** Employers
**Requirements:** The law firm Zafo Law says any Spanish employment contract has to be written in Spanish. A contract can be drafted in two languages, but the Spanish text prevails. The firm doesn't name the statute behind the rule. [20]

### Italy
**Requirement level:** No specific requirement
**Applies to:** Employers
**Requirements:** Italian law doesn't impose specific rules on the language of employment contracts. A contract may be in any language both parties understand, but the Italian translation prevails in legal proceedings. [15]

### Poland
**Requirement level:** Mandatory (Polish prevails; foreign employees can choose another language)
**Applies to:** Employers
**Requirements:** Under the Act on the Polish Language, when employment documents for a Polish employee are bilingual, the Polish version always prevails. Since October 15, 2009, documents for an employee who isn't a Polish citizen may be prepared in a foreign language the employee speaks. The employer has to tell the employee about the right to documents in Polish, and the employee's choice of language is then binding. [21]

### Austria, Bulgaria, Croatia, Czech Republic and Hungary
**Requirement level:** No specific law
**Applies to:** Employers
**Requirements:** The laws of these five countries don't require employment documents in their national languages. The law firm Wolf Theiss advises preparing them in a language all employees fully understand. [22]

### Romania
**Requirement level:** Mandatory for contracts (Romanian)
**Applies to:** Employers
**Requirements:** Only the employment agreement must be concluded in Romanian. The accompanying employment documents don't have to be, and the law doesn't regulate or prohibit particular languages for communications within a company. [22]

### Slovenia
**Requirement level:** Mandatory (Slovenian required)
**Applies to:** Employers
**Requirements:** The employment agreement and all employment documents must be in Slovenian, or in a bilingual or multilingual format that includes Slovenian. Internal communication about employees' rights and obligations, and instructions and information to employees, including on occupational safety, must also be in Slovenian. [22]

### Other Europe
**Requirement level:** Varies
**Applies to:** Employers
**Requirements:** **Switzerland:** no language requirements. **Denmark:** employers must make sure employees understand. [10] **United Kingdom:** no specific legal rules govern the language of employment contracts. [15]

## Asia-Pacific

### China
**Requirement level:** No specific statutory rule (Chinese version advised)
**Applies to:** Employers
**Requirements:** Chinese law has no specific rule on the language of an employment contract. [23] Chinese courts accept contracts written only in a foreign language, but the court has them translated by institutions it approves. A bilingual contract that doesn't name a controlling language usually defaults to Chinese, and if both versions claim to control, Chinese still gets priority. China Briefing advises having a Chinese version for legal certainty. [24]

### Japan
**Requirement level:** No explicit language rule (employee understanding expected)
**Applies to:** Employers
**Requirements:** Japan requires employers to make sure employees understand. [10] A company that employs ten or more workers on a steady basis must set work rules, file them with the Labor Standards Inspection Office and make them known to employees. The Tokyo Metropolitan Government says it's desirable to do this in a language employees understand if they don't understand Japanese. [25]

### Indonesia
**Requirement level:** Mandatory (Indonesian required; bilingual allowed)
**Applies to:** Employers
**Requirements:** Law No. 24/2009 requires the Indonesian language in agreements, and an agreement with a non-Indonesian party may be bilingual, in Indonesian and another language. [26] Under Article 57 of the Employment Law, fixed-term employment agreements must be in Bahasa Indonesia, and if a bilingual version conflicts, the Indonesian version prevails. [27] A Supreme Court circular of December 29, 2023 says a contract with a foreign party can't be cancelled just because it lacks an Indonesian translation, unless the missing translation was caused by one party's bad faith. [26]

### Malaysia
**Requirement level:** No statutory requirement (except the data privacy notice)
**Applies to:** Employers
**Requirements:** Malaysia has no statutory language requirements for an employment contract, other than the data privacy consent or notice under the Personal Data Protection Act, which must be in both English and Bahasa Malaysia. [28]

### Other Asia-Pacific
**Requirement level:** Varies
**Applies to:** Employers
**Requirements:** **Australia:** no language requirements. **New Zealand and Singapore:** recommended or preferred languages. **India:** employers must make sure employees understand. [10] **Thailand:** work rules must be published in Thai, and in English for foreign workers. [29]

## Middle East & Africa

### United Arab Emirates
**Requirement level:** Mandatory (Arabic and English job offer, plus a language the worker understands)
**Applies to:** Private-sector employers hiring expatriate workers
**Requirements:** A job offer must be in Arabic and English, and in a third language the worker understands. The third language is added at the worker's request from a list of nine. Job offers, employment contracts and annexes are usually in Arabic and English. The contract must be based on the signed offer and submitted to the Ministry of Human Resources and Emiratisation (MoHRE) within 14 days of the employee's arrival. [30]

### Saudi Arabia
**Requirement level:** Mandatory (Arabic required)
**Applies to:** Employers
**Requirements:** Article 9 of the Labor Law makes Arabic the language for data, records, files and employment contracts, and for the instructions an employer gives its workers. If an employer also uses a foreign language, the Arabic text prevails. [31]

### South Africa
**Requirement level:** No set language (employee understanding expected)
**Applies to:** Employers
**Requirements:** South Africa has 12 official languages since South African Sign Language was added. [32] Employers must make sure employees understand. [10] The Code of Good Practice: Dismissal (2025) says an employee facing misconduct allegations should, where reasonably possible, have the chance to converse in a language they're comfortable with. [33]

### Other Middle East & Africa
**Requirement level:** Varies
**Applies to:** Employers
**Requirements:** **Egypt:** employment contracts must be in writing and in Arabic. [34] **Israel:** employers must make sure employees understand. [10]

### What are the key compliance considerations for global organizations?

1. **Start with the mandatory places** - places such as France, Belgium, Quebec, Slovenia, Poland, Indonesia and Saudi Arabia set a language by law for at least some employment documents.

2. **Know which version wins** - where two-language documents are allowed, several places name the winner: French in France, Polish in Poland, Arabic in Saudi Arabia, Indonesian for Indonesian fixed-term contracts, and Chinese by default in China.

3. **Check what happens if you get it wrong** - in Flanders and Wallonia a document in the wrong language is null, in Brussels the employer has to replace it, in France terms that aren't in French can't be used against the employee, and Quebec can fine employers.

4. **Make sure people understand** - some rules ask for understanding rather than a set language, such as OSHA training in the US, contracts in Luxembourg and work rules in Japan.

5. **Look at public-sector and safety rules** - Canada's federal institutions and New Brunswick's provincial institutions have their own language rules, and OSHA sets its own for safety training.

6. **Check for changes** - Quebec's francization rules reached employers with 25 or more employees on June 1, 2025, and Indonesia's Supreme Court set out its position on contracts without an Indonesian version on December 29, 2023.
